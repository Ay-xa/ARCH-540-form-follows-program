ARCH 548 GP1, progress review 1

Just EnoughConditioning as a calibrated gradient

What if conditioning is not a fixed property of a room, but a changing relationship between climate, activity, architecture, and season?

The same question in other places: Vancouver's pop-up plazas and rain-friendly plazas, where public life gathers when the weather allows and thins out when it does not. Photographs 3 to 8 are from the City of Vancouver's Shape Your City gallery.

Photographs 1 and 2 by the author. Photographs 3 to 8: City of Vancouver, Shape Your City, pop-up plazas photo gallery, reproduced for an academic review.

Weather shapes public life here, and buildings answer with one line.

Public life in Vancouver is strongly shaped by weather. Rain and cold in winter can make some public activities hard to sustain. At the same time, buildings usually organise environmental control around one clear boundary between inside and outside.

First observation: different activities need different environments. A person running, a person having coffee and a person working at a laptop for hours do not need the same temperature, wind, rain shelter or stability.

Problem: why does early-stage layout so rarely use the adaptive capacity of different activities to decide how much protection a space needs? Typically, we fix the location, size and use of rooms first, and only then work out how to reach the intended conditions.

The literature that revealed the problem

  • City of Vancouver documents: the public is asking for sheltered, heated places to stay. Hot Tea, Rain City (2018): about 1,200 participants and 96 feedback cards, qualitative. Downtown Public Space Strategy (2021) calls for "year-round" and "rain-friendly" public spaces.
  • Vernacular literature (Oliver, Turan): buildings once mediated climate rather than shutting it out.
  • Mora and Bean (2018): ASHRAE 55 is itself a binary. Mechanically conditioned buildings use PMV; naturally conditioned buildings use the adaptive model; there is nothing in between. The adaptive model also requires an outdoor mean of at least 10 °C, which Vancouver does not meet from about November to April.
  • Hou (2025), Ma (2026): most research on semi-outdoor spaces is in hot, humid climates. Very little addresses a mild, wet winter. This is a gap.
  • Tool literature (Forma, AURA, Li 2026): existing tools go from form to performance, or from plan to airflow. None puts "how much protection an activity needs" into the layout. This is also a gap.

Thesis statement

Conditioned space can be reinterpreted as a calibrated gradient of climatic protection and exposure, where activity-specific environmental needs and microclimatic conditions guide spatial arrangement and protection strategies.

Research question

How can activity-specific environmental requirements inform early-stage spatial organization to reduce the potential for over-conditioning while maintaining acceptable comfort and usability?

Method, and where I am

Critique: not on or off, but five stages

Opportunity: people doing different activities need different environmental conditions. This difference is a design opportunity that spatial organisation has not yet used. Architecture often treats environmental comfort as something provided to space after its organization.

Claim: conditioning is not "on or off" but a five-stage gradient from exposed to controlled, and each activity gets just enough protection. The relationship between activity and climate builds a conditioning gradient, which is then turned into principles for spatial organisation.

Five stages, defined by enclosure

  • exposedno roof
  • protectedroof, with fewer than two walls
  • bufferedroof, with at least two walls
  • temperedfully enclosed, without mechanical conditioning
  • controlledmechanically conditioned

Direction: three fields, one intelligence

Direction: massing determines the level of protection; the level of protection determines which activities a space can host. Spatial strategies then adjust conditions further, helping the space host those activities.

Climate infrastructure hosts spatial behaviour and supports an inhabitable climate threshold; the threshold is derived from behaviour. Where the three overlap is the vernacular intelligence this thesis tries to recover: buildings that mediate climate instead of shutting it out.

How it is tested: for now, rules and simulation are used to compare configurations. Next, I will add AI-assisted explanation, and test actual performance and the limits of use against a baseline and measurements.

Outcome: not a single optimal answer, but a logic that brings environmental performance into design decisions earlier and lets them be compared and checked.

From performance evaluation to performance-informed spatial design.

Vision: from fixed rooms with fixed settings, towards architecture that can change with climate and activity.

Premises from the literature

The research step asks three questions, one for each field of the diagram, and each uses its own method. They are the next three parts of this review.

  • Activity: what environmental conditions do different activities need, and how much variation can they tolerate? Logical argument, built on borrowed correlational studies.
  • Space (architectural framework): which spatial strategies can provide these conditions without relying fully on mechanical systems? Case study, interpretive.
  • Decision (threshold): how can architects compare these strategies early in design and translate environmental performance into layout decisions? Simulation.

Keep scrolling: the three fields open into the three questions.

Activity: what conditions do activities need, and how much variation can they tolerate?

Borrowed correlational studies, used as steps in a logical argument. None of them was made in Vancouver, so every threshold is labelled and replaceable.

P1 The binary is not necessary

Kim (2019): the stage changes what people expect. Under natural ventilation, people tolerate more temperature variation than under air conditioning. People also adjust their clothing more actively than the ASHRAE 55 clothing model predicts, so standards underestimate how much people adapt by themselves.

Hou (2025): when people can come and go freely, they tolerate a wider range. The review cites Nakano et al.: in semi-outdoor spaces, people tolerate a range two to three times wider than PPD predicts, provided they can leave freely and stay for a short time.

Pérez-Fargallo (2025): when people feel too cold or too warm, they first adjust themselves (clothing, hot drinks) before using heating or opening windows. Intermediate stages can rely on people adapting first.

P2 Comfort depends on the activity and the person

Zhang (2024), Lau (2026): comfort varies with activity intensity and with the person. The more intense the activity, the lower the neutral PET; people over 75 have a neutral PET about 4 °C higher.

Cambridge and Sheffield thresholds: borrowed values. Their seasonal mean temperatures are almost the same as Vancouver's, and there is no local PET study for Vancouver.

Nikolopoulou and Lykoudis (2006), two more findings: neutral temperature follows the previous season, so the same weather feels different in autumn and in spring; and people who come by choice tolerate more discomfort than people passing through, working or waiting.

Krüger (2017), Glasgow: the neutral band (PET 10–18 °C) sits below the original PET neutral range (18–23 °C). People in a cool climate accept lower temperatures.

City of Toronto (2025): acceptable UTCI ranges are set by season and time of day. Its design advice separates active areas (shade and ventilation) from seating areas (a choice of sun and shade). It gives no values by activity.

Kenawy and Elkadi (2018), Melbourne: activity intensity is expressed as metabolic rate: sitting 1 met, standing 1.2 met, walking 2 met.

P3 Comfort shapes use, but does not decide it

Zacharias (2001), Eliasson (2007): comfort affects whether people use a space, but does not decide it. In Montreal, the share of people sitting tracks air temperature closely (r = 0.92), but presence is not satisfaction. In Gothenburg, weather explains about half of the variation in attendance. Zacharias also found that at about 20 °C, as many people sit in the sun as in the shade.

Nikolopoulou and Lykoudis (2006): the purpose of a visit changes how much discomfort people accept. Those who come by choice tolerate more; those passing through, working or waiting tolerate less.

Space: which strategies give these conditions without relying fully on mechanical systems?

Case studies, read interpretively. The strategies are being collected as a kit-of-parts; each one is a way of moving a space one stage along the gradient without a machine.

  • Vernacular strategies: veranda or arcade (rain shelter and social edge), courtyard (microclimate), thermal mass, operable envelope.
  • Semi-outdoor strategies for colder climates (Hou 2025): prioritise winter sun, enclose the windward side against winter wind, use a moderately translucent roof, plant deciduous trees.
  • Passive strategies: winter garden as a buffer (Lacaton and Vassal); radiant heat: in semi-outdoor spaces, radiant temperature comes mainly from the surrounding surfaces, so the roof material matters (Bouyer 2007); shade: mean radiant temperature has the largest effect on PET, so shading is the key strategy (Hartabela 2021).
  • Psychological adjustment: a light or glazed roof makes a space feel more like outdoors and lowers expectations (Hou 2025).
  • Rain shelter (City of Vancouver guidelines): canopies 3.0–5.0 m high, with a depth-to-height ratio of about 7:10.

Three sketches from the kit

An oculus that opens to the winter sun and shades the summer sun; kitchen, dining and garden as nested enclosures, each one stage further out; and a site where flows cross a single covered point. The parts are drawn before they are compiled, so that each one can be placed on the gradient.

Status: collecting has started. The strategies are not yet compiled or connected to the tool.

Decision: rules R1–R8

For each activity in each season, all the rules it triggers are found, and the highest stage they ask for becomes its minimum stage. That minimum is "just enough". A space below it cannot host the activity; a space above it is over-conditioned. The first version covers winter.

Select a rule to place it on the gradient. A filled marker is winter, an open marker is summer.

    Testing the argument with the tool

    The purpose is to test whether the theory can produce spatial decisions that are actually useful. The tool is the runnable version of the argument, not the result itself.

    The tool does three things

    • Assigns a level of protection from activity needs and environmental data, using R1–R8.
    • Generates and compares layouts within basic geometric constraints. Test bed: a community centre (Marpole 2021 functional program, Sunset Park site). It is the test bed, not the subject. The plan layers from buffered through tempered to controlled; controlled spaces gather on the winter-sun side.
    • Explains why a space suits or does not suit an activity. Click a room to see which façade it uses, what it is next to, which stage it needs, the rule behind that stage, and the evidence behind the rule.

    What it shows

    • the argument can be written as rules and run;
    • it produces a recognisable layered organisation;
    • every decision can be traced.

    What it does not yet show

    • how much mechanically conditioned area the gradient saves compared with an all-controlled layout (the baseline comparison is the next step);
    • whether the five stages match real levels of thermal protection;
    • whether a tempered space is comfortable in a Vancouver winter.

    One caveat: the current version still weighs compactness and sun before the gradient when choosing a layout. This will change.

    What comes next

    The research step continues. The link between activity and space is still the thinnest part of the argument: more case studies to read, and a kit-of-parts to build out from them, so that each spatial strategy can be placed on the gradient and tested in the tool.

    Design principles (kit-of-parts)

    Each case is turned into a spatial principle that can be reused in new buildings. From 26 community centre plans: the large rooms are visible from the lobby; a through-lobby links street and park; a covered outdoor promenade extends the interior street, which corresponds directly to buffered public space. Next step: turn the collected strategies into a reusable kit-of-parts.

    Two questions for the reviewers

    • Is the gradient convincing without local measurements?
    • Where does the research logic, from activity to space to decision, need to be straightened out?
